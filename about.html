About Critter Daily

Six little games, new every midnight

Critter Daily is six small puzzle games: trivia, words, languages, numbers and maps. Each takes a few minutes, everyone gets the same puzzles on the same date, and each has a storybook critter who cheers you on. They're free to play, with nothing to sign up for, and they work on phones, tablets and computers. You can add Critter Daily to your home screen from Settings, the sliders button on every page.

The games

  • Deep Cut: name the Wikipedia pages nobody reads. Every right answer counts, and the rarer it is, the deeper Digby the mole digs.
  • Mother Tongue: find the mystery language on the world's language family tree, with Pip the parrot, in 20 guesses.
  • Ten Numbers: ten questions with a number for an answer. Guess close, and Nutmeg the squirrel fills her jar with acorns.
  • Burrow: seven letters, one minute. The rarer your words, the deeper Clover the rabbit digs.
  • Crisscross: a 5x5 mini crossword against the clock, while Webster the spider spins her web.
  • Borderline: three maps from history a day. Whose land was this? Homer the homing pigeon flies home as you get close.

Each game has a guide under it, with the rules, how scoring works and a few tips.

How the puzzles are made

Every puzzle is built from open data, so the answers come from sources you can check:

  • Deep Cut's lists come from Wikidata, the free database behind Wikipedia, and how obscure an answer is from Wikipedia's public page-view counts.
  • Mother Tongue's family tree comes from Glottolog, the scholarly catalog of the world's languages.
  • Ten Numbers' questions come from Wikidata and hand-picked facts, each checked against its Wikipedia article.
  • Burrow's and Crisscross's words come from ENABLE, a public-domain word list, with wordfreq to tell common words from rare ones. Crisscross's clues are written for it.
  • Borderline's borders come from Cliopatria, the Seshat Global History Databank's atlas of the world's states from 3400 BCE to today.

Each game's days are planned a year ahead, the same for everyone. The puzzles are checked before they go out, and one found to be wrong is taken out of the days to come. The Notes go into more detail, game by game.

Your privacy

There are no accounts. Your results, streaks and settings stay in your browser, on your device. When you finish a day's game, your score (and in Deep Cut, your answers) is sent without anything that identifies you, so the results can show how everyone did. The site counts visits to see how it's doing, and ads help keep the games free; in the EU, the UK and Switzerland you're asked first. The privacy page has the details.
