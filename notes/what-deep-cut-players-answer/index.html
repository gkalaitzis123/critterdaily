What 58,000 Deep Cut answers reveal

November 18, 2026 · 5 minute read · about Deep Cut

Deep Cut asks for the right answer nobody else thinks of. Since late October it has also counted, anonymously, which answers players actually give. From Deep Cut #11 on October 27 to #32 on November 17, that came to 57,804 answers to 173 prompts, and they show a lot about how people search their memory.

Where the numbers come from

When you finish a day's Deep Cut, the game sends the answer that counted for each prompt, plus any guesses that weren't on its list, with nothing that identifies you (the privacy page has the details). This note uses only the totals: how many players named each answer, and the wrong guesses at least two people tried. It covers the 173 prompts that at least 50 people played. Across them, 81% of answers counted; the rest of the time, no answer on the list was found.

When players know the whole list

On lists everybody knows, players skip the obvious answer, and many skip it the same way. For "Name a US state", the most common answer wasn't California or Texas but North Dakota, named by 10% of players, ahead of Rhode Island and Wyoming at 7% each. For "Name a country in Europe" it was Liechtenstein (13%), then Montenegro and Andorra. Bhutan led the countries in Asia, Luxembourg the countries that use the euro, and Suriname (14%) the countries that border Brazil, just ahead of Argentina (13%) and Uruguay (11%). Among US presidents, William Howard Taft was named about as often as Franklin Delano Roosevelt, by 9% of players each.

It's a good instinct, and sharing it costs nothing. Deep Cut measures how deep an answer is by how many people read its Wikipedia article, not by how many players pick it, so North Dakota digs the full 50 meters even when one player in ten names it.

When players know one answer

On lists most people only half know, the opposite happens: players give the one answer they're sure of, and it's nearly always among the shallowest on the list.

  • 44% of players named the emperor penguin, out of 42 penguins on the list.
  • 44% named the Grand Canyon for "Name a canyon or gorge".
  • 43% named Marie Curie for "Name a woman scientist".
  • 39% named the Milky Way and 38% the Andromeda Galaxy for "Name a galaxy".
  • 38% named the Hubble Space Telescope for "Name a space telescope".
  • 35% named the Battle of Waterloo for "Name a battle of the Napoleonic Wars".
  • 34% named Michelangelo for "Name a sculptor", and 34% the Treaty of Versailles for "Name a treaty".
  • 33% named Triceratops and 23% Stegosaurus for "Name a horned or armored dinosaur".

Every one of those digs between 1 and 5 meters. The first answer that comes to you is usually the first answer that comes to everyone, because it's the one the world reads about most.

The prompts that stumped most players

A few prompts left most players without an answer that counted. Only 22% found a prehistoric sea reptile on the list, mostly mosasaurs and plesiosaurs. 34% found a battle of World War I, 35% a work by Picasso, 39% a painting by Monet (27% of all players named Water Lilies), 41% a nebula, 42% a winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature and 43% something in science named after a person. Fewer than half named a castle in Britain, a battle of the American Revolutionary War or a mountain in Switzerland.

What these have in common is that people know the topic but not the names on the list. Plenty of players know Picasso painted Guernica (9% named it), but far fewer can name a second work. Most people know the Alps, and many know the Matterhorn (23% named it), but few can go further.

The wrong guesses hundreds of people try

Deep Cut forgives small typos and accepts common other names, but some guesses just aren't on the list. These were tried by the most players:

  • "Phantom of the Opera", by 86 players, for "Name an opera". It's a musical. Carmen, La bohème and The Magic Flute were all on the list.
  • "Crocodile" (75 players) and "alligator" (29) for "Name a prehistoric sea reptile". Both are still around.
  • "Jack Sparrow" (74) for "Name a pirate". He's a film character, and the list is of real pirates, like Blackbeard.
  • "Milky Way" (69) for "Name a nebula". It's a galaxy.
  • "Alps" (57) for "Name a mountain in Switzerland". It's a range, not a mountain.
  • "Dora" (54) for "Name an explorer". Nearly 500 real explorers were on the list.
  • "Zeus" (45) for "Name a Roman god or goddess". He's Greek; his Roman counterpart is Jupiter.
  • "Chile" (41) for "Name a country that borders Brazil". Brazil borders every country in South America except Chile and Ecuador.
  • "Starry Night" (41) for "Name a work by Picasso". That's van Gogh.
  • "Moth" (41) for "Name a butterfly".
  • "Cleopatra" (40) for "Name an Egyptian god or goddess". She was a queen.
  • "Chimpanzee" (39), "gorilla" (37) and "orangutan" (36) for "Name a monkey". All three are apes.
  • "Toronto" (39) for "Name a Canadian province or territory". It's a city, the capital of Ontario.
  • "Taj Mahal" (37) for "Name a palace". It's a mausoleum.
  • "Pompeii" (36) for "Name a volcano in Italy". It's the Roman town that Vesuvius buried.
  • "Picasso" (36) for "Name a French painter". He was Spanish, though he spent most of his life in France.
  • "Marie Curie" (33) for "Name a person who won the Nobel Peace Prize". She won two Nobel Prizes, in physics and chemistry, but not that one.
  • "Plato" (32) and "Neil Armstrong" (30) for "Name an astronomer".

First names on their own came up a lot too. "John" and "James" were each tried by 30 to 44 players on prompts about people, from the legends of King Arthur to the generals of the American Civil War. A first name alone isn't an answer, since it could be almost anyone.

The deepest answers that counted

At the other end, some players went very deep. The rarest answer that counted in these three weeks was Cristóbal Diatristán de Acuña, a 17th-century Spanish Jesuit who traveled down the Amazon, for "Name an explorer": only about 1 in 56,000 people reading the explorers' articles reads his. Other deep finds included Latady Island, off the coast of Antarctica (1 in 51,000, for "Name an island"), and Albert Hull, the American physicist who invented the magnetron (1 in 29,000, for "Name an inventor").

What this means for your next dig

  • Know the whole list? Go quiet anyway. Other players picking North Dakota doesn't make it any shallower.
  • Know one answer? Look for a second. The emperor penguin digs 1 meter; the yellow-eyed penguin digs 23.
  • Read the prompt twice. An opera isn't a musical, an ape isn't a monkey, and a Greek god isn't a Roman one.
  • Use the whole name. "John" could be anyone; "John Adams" is an answer.
