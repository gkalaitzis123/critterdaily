5,000 years of borders: where Borderline's maps come from

November 16, 2026 · 2 minute read · about Borderline

Every day, Borderline shows you three pieces of land on maps of particular years and asks whose they were. Behind them is an open atlas of the world's states, year by year, for more than 5,000 years. Here's how it becomes a puzzle.

An atlas of the world's states

Borderline's borders come from Cliopatria, published by the Seshat Global History Databank under an open license (CC BY 4.0). It traces the land of the world's states, empires and kingdoms from 3400 BCE to today, as shapes that change from one period to the next. It's a scholarly project, and it's frank about its limits: borders long ago are uncertain, and the atlas shows one version of them.

Choosing the mysteries

Not every state makes a good puzzle. A mystery has to be:

  • Big enough to see. At least 50,000 square kilometers, about the size of Costa Rica or Bosnia and Herzegovina, both of which are in the game.
  • Known well enough. Its Wikipedia article has to be read a fair amount, so the answers are places people have heard of, or can work out from the hints.
  • A state, not an age. The atlas also marks periods and groupings, like "Warring States China", which are eras rather than answers, so they're left out.
  • In its own time. Where Wikipedia gives a state's dates, the map's year falls inside them, so you won't meet the Holy Roman Empire before 962.

A place usually appears at its biggest, in the stretch of years when it held the most land. Famous places come back in other ages, when their land was different: the Byzantine Empire, the Kingdom of France, the Kingdom of England, Babylonia and the Ethiopian Empire each appear in four different years. Today's countries appear too, apart from the ones everyone knows at a glance, like Italy or Japan.

From 3000 BCE to 2024

This year's schedule has 489 maps of 424 places. The oldest is Egypt in 3000 BCE, in its Early Dynastic Period; the newest are today's countries. The biggest is the Mongol Empire in 1260, at almost 26 million square kilometers. The smallest, at just over 50,000, is Caucasian Albania around the year 100, an ancient kingdom in what's now Azerbaijan.

Easiest first

Each day's three are ordered by how easy they're likely to be: how well known each place is, with today's countries counted a little easier (everyone has seen today's map) and places in another age a little harder. The first map comes from the easiest third, the second from the middle third and the last from the hardest. A day's three come from different places and, where possible, different parts of the world, and no place comes back within a month.

A year of days

There are new maps every day until April 3, 2027. After that, maps come back in new threes, each at least four months after it was last shown, so a whole day never repeats. The newest maps were checked by hand before they went in, and a few were taken out: the Gallic Empire in 275, a year after it fell, or Denmark–Norway in 1814, drawn after Norway had been ceded to Sweden, which left a map that was mostly Iceland.
