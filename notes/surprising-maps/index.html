Six surprising maps from Borderline

November 16, 2026, updated November 20 · 5 minute read · about Borderline

Borderline's hardest moments come when a famous name had a shape nobody expects. Here are six maps from this year's schedule that look nothing like the places you know, and the history behind them.

Spain, 1670

In 1670 the Spanish crown ruled far more of Europe than Spain. Besides most of the Iberian peninsula it held the Kingdom of Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, the Duchy of Milan, the Franche-Comté and the Spanish Netherlands around Brussels: inheritances of the Habsburg family, which had ruled Spain since the early 1500s. On Borderline's map, Spain's land runs from Madrid to the heel of Italy and north to Brussels. Guess "Italy" and the game tells you it didn't exist yet: Italy wasn't unified until 1861.

Kingdom of England, 1420

A century into the Hundred Years' War, England was at the height of its power in France. After Henry V's victory at Agincourt in 1415 and the Treaty of Troyes in 1420, which made him heir to the French throne, the English crown held Normandy and much of northern France, as well as Gascony in the southwest. About a seventh of the map's land is in today's France, and another tenth in Ireland, around the English-held Pale.

Republic of Venice, 1710

Venice was a city that ruled a sea. Besides its mainland territory in northeastern Italy, in 1710 it held the coast of Dalmatia and the Peloponnese, won from the Ottoman Empire in 1699. It lost the Peloponnese again in 1715, so the map catches a brief moment: half its land is in today's Italy, a third in Greece and the rest in Croatia.

Adal Sultanate, 1540

For about fifteen years, the Adal Sultanate, centered on the eastern lowlands around Harar, conquered most of the Ethiopian highlands under its leader, Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim. Ethiopia, with Portuguese help, defeated him in 1543. The map from 1540 shows Adal at its peak: four-fifths of its land is in today's Ethiopia, and the rest in Eritrea, Somalia and Djibouti.

Brunei, 1700

Today Brunei is a small country of about 5,800 square kilometers on the coast of Borneo. Its sultanate was once a great trading power, and at its height in the 1500s its influence reached the Philippines. On Borderline's map for 1700 it still covers about 68,700 square kilometers along Borneo's northern coast, roughly twelve times its size today.

Babylonia, four times

Babylonia appears in Borderline in four different years: in 1760 BCE, under Hammurabi; in 1400 BCE, under the Kassite kings; in 1100 BCE; and in 650 BCE, when it was ruled from Assyria, so that year the Neo-Assyrian Empire counts as a right answer too. It's the same name on four different shapes, spread over more than 1,100 years. If you see southern Mesopotamia shaded and the year is BCE, Babylonia is always worth a guess.

Why these shapes surprise

Each of the six breaks a habit of reading maps. We expect a country to be one block of land around its capital, but Spain in 1670 was a collection of inheritances, held together by a royal family rather than by borders. England in 1420 was the product of a war and a treaty, holding land across the sea that it would lose within a generation. Venice was a trading power whose land followed its sea routes, so its map is mostly coastline. Adal's conquests lasted barely fifteen years, and Brunei's map shows a sultanate already past its peak. Babylonia is the reverse: one name for a land whose shape kept changing for more than a thousand years.

What to do when a map surprises you

When the shape makes no sense, trust the year over the shape. Ask who was powerful then, not who holds the land today: in 1670 that means the great royal houses of Europe, in 1420 the kingdoms fighting the Hundred Years' War. Guess the biggest state you can think of for that century and region. Even a wrong guess tells you how far away the mystery is, which way to look and whether your guess is too early or too late for the year.

Then let the hints work. After two wrong guesses, Borderline tells you which of today's countries the land is in, or, for a state that still exists, when its modern state began. After three, it names the other states on the map, which often leaves only one gap to fill. A surprising shape is usually a famous name in an unfamiliar year, so once you have the region and the century, the name tends to follow.
