How Deep Cut decides what's obscure

November 16, 2026, updated November 20 · 4 minute read · about Deep Cut

Most trivia games want one right answer. Deep Cut takes any right answer, then rewards you for picking one nobody else would. That needs a fair way to measure how obscure an answer is, and Deep Cut uses Wikipedia.

Page views as a measure of fame

Every answer in Deep Cut has its own English Wikipedia article, and Wikipedia publishes how many times each article is read. Those counts are a good stand-in for how well known something is: famous things are read a lot, and obscure ones hardly at all. Deep Cut looks at the last 60 days, so one busy day doesn't decide anything.

Raw page views can't be compared across lists, though. The article on gold is read about 2,100 times a day, more than most Roman emperors' articles, but nobody would call gold obscure. So Deep Cut only compares an answer with the other answers to the same prompt.

Your answer's share of the readers

For each prompt, Deep Cut adds up the daily readers of every answer on its list, then works out each answer's share. Take "Name a Roman emperor". About 71,000 people a day read the articles of the 143 emperors on the list, and Augustus alone gets about 5,100 of them: roughly 1 in 14. Trebonianus Gallus, who reigned for two years in the middle of the third century, gets about 1 in 290. At the very bottom, a third-century usurper named Septimius gets about 1 in 55,000.

That share is an answer's rarity. The game shows it after each prompt as "1 in" a number: the bigger the number, the deeper the cut.

From rarity to meters

Digby the mole turns rarity into depth. On every list, the most-read answer digs 1 meter and the rarest answers dig 50, and everything between is spaced by how many times rarer it is, so within a list, each time an answer is ten times rarer it digs the same distance further. That keeps the prompts fair. Some lists are full of famous names and some are mostly unknowns, but on each of them the deepest answers reach 50 meters.

Your meters add up over the eight prompts, and Digby digs back through Earth's history: through the Holocene, into the Pleistocene at 96 meters, then the Cretaceous at 128 and the Jurassic at 160, down to the Permian at 200 and the Cambrian at 240.

Why the obvious answer is a trap

The first answer that comes to mind is usually the one everybody thinks of, and that's usually the most-read one. Among the US states, California is read by 1 in 20 of the people reading about states, and digs 1 meter; North Dakota, at 1 in 107, digs 50. Among the chemical elements, gold is 1 in 34 and bohrium 1 in 630. The deep answers aren't trick answers. They're the second and third things you know.

What counts as an answer

Each list is built from Wikidata, the free database behind Wikipedia, and only answers with their own English Wikipedia article count, because that's where their readers are measured. Spelling needn't be perfect: small typos are forgiven, accents and punctuation don't matter, and common other names count too, so Octavian is as good as Augustus.

Readers come and go, so when the lists are refreshed an answer's rarity can shift. Something in the news can suddenly be read a lot, which is one more reason the deepest answers tend to be the quiet ones.
