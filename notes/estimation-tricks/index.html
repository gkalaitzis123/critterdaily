Estimation tricks for Ten Numbers

November 16, 2026, updated November 20 · 4 minute read · about Ten Numbers

Ten Numbers asks questions nobody knows the exact answer to: how many people live in a country, how far apart two cities are, what year a battle was fought. It's a game of estimating, and estimating is a skill you can get better at. Here are five habits, with the points they'd earn under the game's real scoring.

1. Get the size right first

For counts like populations, Ten Numbers measures how far off you are as a ratio. France has about 68.6 million people: guessing 60 million earns 95 points and 80 million earns 94. Even half or double does fine: 34 million earns 65 and 137 million earns 66. But ten times off, 680 million, earns only 15. Before you worry about the digits, decide: thousands, millions or billions?

2. Anchor on something you know

If you know one number nearby, build from it. If you know France has about 68 million people, its smaller neighbors must have a fraction of that, and a city, even a capital, a fraction of its country. Most people know a few numbers well: their own country's population, their city's, the height of a famous building. Each one is a ruler you can hold other numbers against.

3. Turn distances into flight times

An airliner covers roughly 800 to 900 kilometers an hour. A flight from London to New York takes about seven or eight hours, so the distance is something like 5,600 to 6,400 kilometers. The answer, as the crow flies, is 5,570: guessing 6,000 earns 94 points, and 5,000 earns 91. Flights take longer than the straight line, so shave a little off.

4. Place a year between two you're sure of

A year gets room that grows with its age: a few years either side for something recent, and up to 80 for ancient history. For the Battle of Hastings, 1066, guessing 1086 earns 84 points and 1116 earns 57, while a century early, 966, earns 27. If you can place an event before or after something you know, like a war, an invention or a famous reign, you can usually get within a few decades.

5. Never skip

Points fall smoothly the further off you are but never quite reach zero, and astronomical numbers get extra room. For the number of ants on Earth, about 20 quadrillion, a guess ten times too high or too low still earns 76 points. A skip earns nothing, so a rough guess always beats no guess.

Five past questions, worked through

Here are five questions from recent days, with the habit that cracks each one and the points it earns. None needs a fact you're unlikely to know.

  • How many people live in Italy? (#34, November 19) Anchor on a neighbor: France has about 68.6 million people, and Italy somewhat fewer. Guess 60 million and you earn 100 points: the answer is 58.85 million.
  • How far is it from Dubai to London, as the crow flies? (#31, November 16) The flight takes about seven hours, and at 800 kilometers an hour that's 5,600 kilometers. The answer is 5,470, so 5,600 earns 99 points.
  • In what year did Texas become a US state? (#30, November 15) Place it between two dates you know. Texas won its independence from Mexico in 1836, the year of the Alamo, and was a republic of its own for about a decade before it joined the Union. Guess 1846 and you earn 99 points; the answer is 1845.
  • How many people live in Luxembourg? (#28, November 13) Get the size right first. It's a small country, smaller than many cities, so think hundreds of thousands, not millions. The answer is about 691,000: 500,000 earns 85 points, and 5 million, the size of a mid-sized country, earns only 21.
  • How many years old is the earliest evidence of life on Earth? (#29, November 14) Anchor on the Earth itself, about 4.5 billion years old, and remember that life began early in its history. The answer is about 3.8 billion: a guess of 3 billion earns 92 points, and even 1 billion earns 50.

The same few habits carry every one of them: decide the size, find a number you already know, and adjust from there.

Why the scoring works this way

Ten Numbers' points follow a smooth curve, fitted to real players' first days so that a typical day scores about 450 out of 1,000. A curve that fell off a cliff would only reward people who happen to know a number; this one rewards getting close, which is the skill. After each guess, the number line shows the green band that would have earned 70 points or more, so you can see how much room each kind of question gives.
