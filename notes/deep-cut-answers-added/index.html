The answers players typed that Deep Cut now counts

November 20, 2026 · 4 minute read · about Deep Cut

Deep Cut takes any right answer, but only if it's on the prompt's list, so a list with a gap turns a right answer away. In November, all 254 lists were rebuilt from Wikidata and Wikipedia and then checked against every guess the game had turned down in its first month. Of the guesses at least two players tried, 461 different ones would count today. Between them, they had been typed 1,968 times.

Answers that were missing

Some lists were narrower than their prompts. "Name a garden plant" had no tomatoes, ferns or basil. "Name something in science named after a person" had 175 answers but none of the chemical elements named after scientists, like einsteinium and curium; it now has 468. Here are some of the guesses tried most often that count now.

Other names

Many more were right answers under another name. Brand names now count for the medicines they're sold as, so Advil is ibuprofen and Calpol is paracetamol. Trees count by the names people use, not only their articles' Latin ones: the weeping willow is Salix babylonica, the red oak Quercus rubra and the cherry blossom Prunus serrulata. And "Conan", from the manga Detective Conan, counts as Shinichi Kudo, the detective's real name in the story.

Lists as long as the world

Behind 189 of the lists there's now a full list as well, about 456,000 answers in all: every island with a Wikipedia article, every city in the countries and states Deep Cut has city prompts for, every drug with an ATC code (the international system for classifying medicines), every museum, bridge and skyscraper with an article, and every species in the tree list's genera. A full list never changes the prompt's main list. It means that if you know a real answer too obscure to be on it, it still counts, and it usually digs deep.

Uncertain, a city of 85 people in East Texas, now digs 50 meters for "Name a city in Texas". So does Serra da Saudade, which, with 833 people, is the least populated municipality in Brazil, for "Name a city in Brazil". Answers from a full list have to be spelled right, because a list that long would catch too many typos.

What no longer counts

The checking went the other way too. Some answers counted for a single word that could mean almost anyone. Now a lone word that's an everyday word, a common first name or a common surname counts only when it's what the answer is usually called, or when the answer is well known: no deeper than 30 meters, or read at least 100 times a day. "Taylor" still counts for Zachary Taylor, the 12th president, whose article is read more than 1,800 times a day. "Smith" no longer counts for James Smith, one of the signers of the Declaration of Independence, whose article is read a few dozen times a day at most.

Answers that went missing, and came back

A rebuilt list can lose answers as well as gain them. When Wikidata, the database the lists come from, renames or reorganizes an entry, an answer can drop out. The November rebuild lost a few, among them the Egyptian pyramids from "Name an archaeological site" and the astronaut Victor Glover, and both are back. Every rebuild now lists any well-known answer a list has lost, so it can be checked before players notice.

What still doesn't count

Some guesses will never count, because they aren't answers to the prompt: the Phantom of the Opera isn't an opera, Jack Sparrow isn't a real pirate and a chimpanzee isn't a monkey. What 58,000 Deep Cut answers reveal has more of them.

What this means when you play

  • Use the name you know. Brand names, common names and nicknames count when they mean one answer.
  • Go obscure, and spell it right. A real answer too rare for the main list still counts from the full list, usually at depth.
  • Use whole names for people. "Taylor" works for a president; for most people, a surname alone could be anyone.
